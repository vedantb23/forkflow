  The real problem: it's the uniqueness that's weak, not the tech

  "Swiggy clone" is what every candidate submits. An interviewer sees the title
  and mentally files it as generic before you speak. Your CP profile + IIT ISM
  tag deserve better than a project that reads as a tutorial follow-along.

  But here's the key insight: you don't fix uniqueness by changing the tech. You
  fix it with a sharp angle that changes what the product is, while keeping all
  the concurrency/real-time/RAG meat.

  3 angles that keep the tech, kill the "generic" smell

  A) Kitchen-capacity-aware ordering (my top pick).
  Real kitchens can only cook ~N orders per 15-min window. Model that. Now
  ordering food becomes a Ticketmaster-style capacity-allocation problem —
  you're not just decrementing stock, you're allocating limited throughput 
  slots. Two orders competing for the last slot in the 1:00–1:15 window = a
  genuinely harder race than "last burger." Almost nobody builds this. It makes
  prep-time estimates real instead of fake, and gives you a killer interview
  story: "I modeled the kitchen as a bounded-capacity resource per time-window."

  B) Campus food platform (IIT ISM messes/canteens).
  Same as your CollegeRush instinct that you liked — personal story, a problem
  you've lived, zero other candidates have it. Lunch rush = natural concurrency
  spike. Mess/canteen capacity = the slot problem above, for free. "Built to fix
  the actual chaos at my institute's canteens."

  C) Tiffin / home-chef subscription marketplace.
  Adds recurring orders (a scheduled-job/BullMQ-cron problem generic clones
  never touch) + home cooks with daily fixed capacity. Different enough that it
  doesn't read as Swiggy.

  Campus food-ordering platform with kitchen-capacity-aware slots (A + B 
  combined). It's unique, it's a real story, and the capacity model upgrades the
  concurrency problem from "inventory decrement" to "bounded throughput
